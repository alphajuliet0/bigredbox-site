The Geopolitical Cyber Risk Hiding in Your Network Edge

26 May 2026

When people hear about state-linked cyber activity, it can sound like a problem for governments, telecoms firms and critical national infrastructure. That is a dangerous assumption.

The latest guidance from the UK National Cyber Security Centre and international partners points to a practical issue that affects much smaller organisations too: attackers are making heavy use of exposed edge infrastructure. That means routers, firewalls, VPN appliances, remote access gateways and internet-facing devices that sit between your business and the outside world.

For a UK SME, this is not about becoming a direct target of a foreign intelligence service. It is about being reachable, useful and under-maintained. If an edge device is exposed, unpatched or poorly monitored, it can become a route into your network, a stepping stone into a customer or supplier, or part of a wider covert network used to hide hostile activity.

Why Edge Devices Matter

Your network edge is supposed to be the controlled doorway into the business. It is where remote workers connect, where branch offices link back, where suppliers may be allowed in, and where cloud or managed service access often begins.

That makes it valuable. If an attacker compromises a laptop, they still need to move around. If they compromise a VPN gateway, firewall or router, they may already be standing at the doorway with a view of who comes in, where traffic goes and which internal systems are reachable.

The NCSC’s April 2026 advisory on China-nexus covert networks describes a shift towards large networks of compromised routers and other edge devices. Earlier CISA guidance has also warned about state-sponsored actors targeting routers and using trusted connections to move into other networks. The important point for business leaders is simple: edge devices are no longer boring plumbing. They are high-value security assets.

What This Means for SMEs

Most smaller businesses do not run their own security operations centre. Many rely on a managed IT provider, a firewall installed years ago, a VPN set up during remote working, or a router supplied as part of a connectivity contract.

That is normal. The risk appears when nobody has clear ownership of those devices after installation.

Common weak spots include:

  • Old firewall or VPN firmware that has missed security updates.
  • Remote management interfaces exposed to the internet.
  • Shared admin passwords or accounts that are no longer tied to named people.
  • No multi-factor authentication on remote access.
  • Limited logging, which means suspicious access is hard to reconstruct.
  • Supplier access that remains open long after it is needed.

None of these require a dramatic failure. They are usually the result of small operational gaps that build up over time.

The Commercial Risk Is Wider Than a Breach

The business impact is not limited to data theft. A compromised edge device can affect continuity, customer confidence, insurance conversations and supplier due diligence.

If your business provides services to larger organisations, your cyber posture may increasingly be judged by how well you control remote access, patch internet-facing systems and monitor privileged entry points. A weak edge can become a procurement problem as much as a technical one.

There is also a supply chain angle. Attackers often look for the easiest useful route, not the biggest brand name. A smaller firm with trusted access into a larger customer, finance platform, logistics partner or professional services network can be valuable even if it holds little obvious sensitive data itself.

What Directors Should Ask This Week

This does not need to start with a complex security project. It should start with a clear ownership conversation. Ask your IT provider or internal lead for a short written answer to these questions:

  • What internet-facing routers, firewalls, VPNs and remote access services do we have?
  • Who owns patching and configuration changes for each one?
  • Are management interfaces restricted, or can they be reached from the public internet?
  • Is multi-factor authentication enforced for all remote access?
  • When were the devices last updated, and are any out of vendor support?
  • Are logs being collected somewhere useful, and who reviews alerts?
  • Which suppliers have remote access, and when was that access last reviewed?

If the answer is unclear, that is the first risk to fix. You cannot protect what nobody can list.

A Practical Edge Security Baseline

For most SMEs, the sensible baseline is achievable:

  • Keep an asset list: record every firewall, router, VPN appliance and remote access service, including model, owner, support status and management route.
  • Patch quickly: treat edge device updates as urgent because these systems are directly exposed.
  • Turn off exposed management: admin portals should not be open to the whole internet.
  • Require MFA: remote access without multi-factor authentication should be treated as a business risk, not an IT preference.
  • Restrict access: use allow lists where practical, especially for admin access and supplier connections.
  • Review supplier access: remove old accounts, name every privileged user and agree how emergency access is approved.
  • Collect logs: make sure firewall, VPN and admin activity can be reviewed after an incident.
  • Replace unsupported kit: if a device no longer receives security updates, plan its removal.

These measures will not stop every advanced attacker, but they remove the avoidable weaknesses that make a business easy to use as a foothold.

Where Cyber Essentials Fits

The NCSC continues to point organisations towards Cyber Essentials because it covers the basics that matter here: firewalls, secure configuration, security updates, access control and malware protection.

For many SMEs, Cyber Essentials is a useful way to turn a vague security concern into a practical checklist. It also gives directors a clearer way to ask whether the business has done the simple things properly.

The bigredbox Take

Geopolitical cyber risk can sound remote, but the practical exposure is often local and visible. It sits in the devices that connect your business to the internet and let people work remotely.

You do not need to track every named threat group to make progress. You need to know what is exposed, keep it supported, restrict who can reach it, enforce MFA and make sure someone is watching the logs.

If your firewall, VPN or router has become invisible because it usually works, bring it back onto the management agenda. The network edge is now part of the board-level cyber risk picture.

Useful references: NCSC guidance on China-nexus covert networks, CISA advisory on compromised networks and routers, and NCSC Cyber Essentials.
