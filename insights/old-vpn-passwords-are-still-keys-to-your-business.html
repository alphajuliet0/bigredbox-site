Old VPN Passwords Are Still Keys to Your Business

11 July 2026

Recent FortiBleed reporting is a useful reminder of an uncomfortable truth: an old VPN or firewall password can stay dangerous long after everyone thinks the original issue has been fixed.

The public reporting focuses on Fortinet and FortiGate credentials, with tens of thousands of firewall and VPN accounts reportedly exposed or targeted through credential harvesting and brute-force activity. Some reports also describe UK public-sector exposure. The brand name matters if you use that technology, but the wider lesson is bigger than one vendor.

If a remote access password has been stolen, reused, cracked or left unchanged after an incident, patching the appliance does not remove the risk. The door may be stronger, but someone may still have a key.

Why This Matters for SMEs

Many SMEs rely on a firewall, VPN or remote access gateway that is managed by an IT provider. It may have been installed years ago and quietly left to do its job. That is exactly why these systems are valuable to attackers. They sit on the edge of the network, they are reachable from the internet and they often have a route towards internal systems.

When attackers obtain valid credentials, they may not need to exploit a new vulnerability. They can try to log in like an authorised user. If the account has broad permissions, if multi-factor authentication is missing, or if old accounts are still enabled, the business can be exposed without an obvious malware alert.

This is not only a technical problem. It is a business continuity problem. A compromised firewall or VPN can lead to email compromise, ransomware staging, data theft, altered security settings and hidden backdoor accounts.

Patching Is Necessary, but IT Is Not Enough

Security updates close known weaknesses in the software. They do not automatically invalidate every password, token, local admin account or integration that may have been exposed before the update.

After any serious edge-device warning, ask two separate questions:

  • Is the device fully patched and supported?
  • Have all relevant credentials and access paths been reviewed since the risk became known?

If the answer to the second question is no, the work is incomplete.

What to Check This Week

1. Identify Your Internet-Facing Remote Access

List the systems that allow remote access into the business. This may include VPNs, firewalls, remote desktop gateways, cloud admin portals, remote monitoring tools and supplier support platforms. If your IT provider manages this, ask for the list in writing.

2. Rotate High-Risk Credentials

Change passwords for firewall and VPN administrator accounts, remote access users, service accounts and any directory accounts linked to the appliance. Do not only change the obvious admin password. Check whether the device stores LDAP, Active Directory, RADIUS or API credentials as well.

3. Enforce Multi-Factor Authentication

Remote access without MFA should be treated as a priority risk. MFA will not solve every problem, but it reduces the value of stolen passwords and makes credential stuffing less effective.

4. Remove Old and Shared Accounts

Disable accounts for former staff, old suppliers, test users and emergency access that no longer has a clear owner. Shared accounts make investigation difficult because nobody can prove who used them.

5. Review Login History

Look for unusual countries, unfamiliar IP addresses, unexpected times, failed login spikes and successful logins by accounts that should not be active. If logs are not available, that is a finding in itself.

6. Restrict Management Interfaces

The management interface for a firewall or VPN should not be exposed to the internet unless there is a strong, documented reason. Where possible, restrict administration to trusted IP addresses, a separate management network or a secure access process.

7. Check for Persistence

If there is any sign of compromise, do not stop at changing passwords. Check for new local admin users, modified firewall rules, altered VPN settings, changed logging, new certificates, scheduled tasks and suspicious integrations. In some cases, replacement or full rebuild may be safer than trusting the existing configuration.

Questions to Ask Your IT Provider

  • Which firewall, VPN or remote access products do we use?
  • Are they still supported and fully patched?
  • Have all administrator and VPN credentials been rotated recently?
  • Is MFA enforced for every remote access route?
  • Is the management interface exposed to the internet?
  • Can you show recent login activity and failed login attempts?
  • What would we do if the appliance itself was compromised?

A good provider should be able to answer these questions plainly. If they cannot, that does not automatically mean the environment is unsafe, but it does mean the risk is not being managed visibly.

Do Not Wait for Your Vendor Name to Appear in the News

FortiBleed is the current headline, but the same pattern applies across edge technology. VPNs, firewalls and remote access systems are repeatedly targeted because they are valuable, exposed and often trusted too much.

The safer approach is to treat edge credentials as perishable. Rotate them when there is a credible risk, remove access that is no longer required and make sure remote administration is limited and monitored.

The bigredbox Take

Old credentials are not history. They are active risk if they still work.

For SMEs, the immediate action is simple: know what remote access exists, confirm it is patched, rotate the right credentials, enforce MFA and review who has used it. This is not about buying another tool. It is about taking back control of the keys to the business.

If your firewall or VPN is managed by someone else, ask the questions now. Remote access should be convenient for your business, not convenient for an attacker.
