When Opening the Email Is Enough

27 July 2026

Most phishing advice assumes the user has to click something. Check the link. Look at the sender. Be careful with attachments. That advice still matters, but it does not cover every modern email attack.

The NCSC and international partners have warned about Russian military intelligence activity targeting logistics and technology organisations, including zero-click exploitation against Zimbra webmail. In a zero-click attack, compromise may happen when a message is processed or viewed. The victim does not have to open a suspicious attachment or type a password into a fake site.

For UK SMEs, the lesson is straightforward: phishing awareness is useful, but it is not a complete email security strategy.

Why This Matters Beyond Zimbra

The current reporting names Zimbra because that is where this campaign has been observed. If your organisation uses Zimbra, or a supplier hosts Zimbra on your behalf, this should be checked urgently.

But the broader point applies to any business email platform. Email is still one of the most valuable routes into a company. It carries invoices, supplier conversations, contracts, customer details, reset links and internal decisions. If the mail system is vulnerable, attackers may not need to trick an employee in the traditional sense.

This is where many SMEs have a blind spot. They know who uses email, but not who owns the mail platform, who patches it, who monitors it and what would happen if it was compromised.

Awareness Training Has Limits

Staff training should continue. People still need to report suspicious emails, unexpected login prompts, invoice changes and unusual requests. But it is unfair and unsafe to make staff the only line of defence.

A user cannot spot a hidden server-side exploit. A finance assistant cannot patch a webmail platform. A director cannot review mail server logs if nobody collects them. Good security needs technical controls and supplier accountability around the inbox.

The right question is not, Are staff careful? It is, Would our mail environment still be defensible if staff never had a chance to click or not click?

What SMEs Should Check This Week

1. Know What Runs Your Email

Document the mail platform in use, including hosted webmail, Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, Zimbra, cPanel mail, Exchange, third-party spam filtering and any archiving or backup service. If an IT provider manages it, ask for the list in writing.

2. Confirm Patch Ownership

For self-hosted or supplier-hosted mail platforms, confirm who applies security updates and how quickly critical patches are installed. If the answer is vague, treat that as a risk. Email servers and webmail interfaces should not be left on a best-efforts patching model.

3. Check Whether Zimbra Is Present

Do not assume you know. Zimbra may be used by a supplier, legacy hosting platform or specialist service. Ask directly: do we use Zimbra anywhere, including hosted webmail or customer support mailboxes?

4. Review Logs and Alerts

Useful signs include unusual mailbox access, new forwarding rules, suspicious webmail sessions, unexpected administrator activity, mass downloads, unknown IP addresses and disabled logging. If logs are not available, that is a finding.

5. Protect Mailbox Administrators

Mailbox administrators can reset access, search mail, change rules and alter security settings. These accounts should use strong MFA, should not be shared and should not be used for everyday browsing and email.

6. Back Up What Matters

Email backup is often misunderstood. Cloud mail does not always mean you have a recoverable independent backup. Check whether you can restore deleted mailboxes, recover from account compromise and export key mail records if the platform is unavailable.

7. Ask About Detection, Not Just Filtering

Spam filtering is not the same as compromise detection. Ask how your provider detects suspicious mailbox activity after an email gets through or after a platform-level issue is disclosed.

Questions for Your IT Provider

  • Which email platforms and webmail services do we use?
  • Is Zimbra used anywhere in our environment or supply chain?
  • Who patches the mail platform, and what is the critical patch timescale?
  • What mailbox activity do you monitor?
  • Can we see recent sign-in and webmail access logs?
  • How are mailbox administrator accounts protected?
  • Can we restore email independently if the platform is compromised?
  • Would you tell us if a mail server vulnerability affected us?

These are reasonable questions. If your provider cannot answer them clearly, the business does not have enough visibility over a critical system.

What to Do If You Suspect Compromise

Act quickly and preserve evidence. Do not only reset one password and move on.

  1. Disable suspicious sessions and reset affected credentials.
  2. Revoke mailbox sessions and review MFA devices.
  3. Check forwarding rules, inbox rules and delegated access.
  4. Review administrator activity and recent configuration changes.
  5. Check whether sensitive emails, attachments or finance conversations were accessed.
  6. Confirm the platform is patched before returning to normal operation.
  7. Escalate to your IT provider, insurer or incident support contact if there is evidence of unauthorised access.

If exploitation may have happened at the mail platform level, assume more than one mailbox could be affected until logs prove otherwise.

The bigredbox Take

Email security cannot depend entirely on users spotting bad messages. Zero-click activity shows why the platform itself, and the supplier managing it, must be part of the control set.

For SMEs, the priority is not to become experts in every named vulnerability. The priority is to know what email systems you run, ensure they are patched, protect administrator access, monitor mailbox behaviour and test recovery.

If opening the email can be enough, the defence has to start before the email reaches the user.
