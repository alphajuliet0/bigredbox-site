Seven Security Questions Your IT Supplier Should Be Able to Answer

15 June 2026

Most small businesses do not run their technology alone. An external IT company manages Microsoft 365. A software provider holds customer data. A payroll platform connects to finance. A website agency controls hosting, domains and plug-ins. Each supplier may be useful, capable and trusted, but every connection also becomes part of the organisation's attack surface.

This matters because attackers do not always target the best-protected route. They look for the easiest route into a useful network, account or data set. A supplier with privileged access can offer exactly that.

Recent UK attention on managed service providers and supply chain security is therefore not just a regulatory story. It is a practical warning for every SME that relies on an outside organisation to operate, support or secure its technology.

Trust Is Not a Security Control

A long-standing relationship can create confidence, but it does not prove that access is controlled, monitored or recoverable. The supplier may have excellent engineers and good intentions while still using shared administrator accounts, weak remote access, excessive permissions or incomplete logging.

The useful question is not whether you trust the supplier. It is whether both businesses can demonstrate how that trust is protected.

Start by identifying which suppliers can:

  • Access administrator accounts or security settings;
  • Connect remotely to devices or networks;
  • Read, store or process sensitive information;
  • Change DNS, domains, websites or email configuration;
  • Deploy software or updates across several systems;
  • Create, reset or disable user accounts;
  • Access backups or recovery services.

These are not ordinary suppliers. They are part of your security boundary.

Why Supplier Access Is So Valuable to Attackers

Compromising one well-connected provider can give an attacker access to several customers. It can also make malicious activity appear legitimate because it arrives through a trusted account, management tool or software update process.

For an SME, the consequences can include email compromise, ransomware, stolen customer data, interrupted trading and loss of access to essential systems. The technical incident may begin elsewhere, but the commercial impact lands with you.

Outsourcing a service does not outsource responsibility for understanding the risk. Your supplier should help you manage that responsibility rather than asking you to accept a vague assurance that security is handled.

Seven Questions to Ask Your IT Supplier

1. How Is Privileged Access Protected?

Ask whether every administrator has an individual account, whether multi-factor authentication is enforced and whether privileged access is separated from ordinary email and web browsing. Shared administrator credentials make accountability and incident investigation much harder.

2. What Remote Management Tools Are Used?

You should know which tools can connect to your systems, who can use them and how access is reviewed. Ask whether dormant connections are removed and whether unusual remote activity creates an alert.

3. Can You Show Us What You Can Access?

Request a simple access register. It should list systems, account types, permission levels and the business reason for access. If neither party can describe the access clearly, it is unlikely to be controlled properly.

4. How Quickly Will You Tell Us About an Incident?

Contracts should define notification expectations. You need enough time to protect accounts, inform insurers, preserve evidence and assess legal or regulatory duties. A supplier should not wait for a complete investigation before warning you that your environment may be affected.

5. What Happens If Your Service Is Unavailable?

Ask how you regain control of domains, cloud tenants, backups, licences and administrator accounts if the provider is offline or the relationship ends. Business continuity should not depend on one person's password or one supplier's availability.

6. How Do You Secure Your Own Suppliers?

Your supplier probably relies on other platforms and subcontractors. Ask which services are critical, how they are assessed and how changes are communicated. Supply chain risk rarely stops after one link.

7. What Evidence Can You Provide?

Useful evidence might include Cyber Essentials certification, independent testing, security policies, access review records, backup test results or a clear incident response plan. No single certificate proves that a supplier is secure, but evidence is more valuable than reassurance.

What Good Looks Like

A mature MSP or technology supplier should welcome proportionate questions. It should be able to explain its controls in plain English, identify shared responsibilities and help you reduce unnecessary access.

For most SMEs, the immediate priorities are straightforward:

  1. Create a list of suppliers with access to systems or data.
  2. Identify the accounts and connections each supplier uses.
  3. Require multi-factor authentication for privileged and remote access.
  4. Remove old accounts, unused integrations and excessive permissions.
  5. Agree incident notification and escalation contacts.
  6. Confirm that you can recover essential services without relying on one supplier.
  7. Review the arrangement at least annually and whenever the service changes.

This does not require a large procurement department. It requires ownership, a reliable record and a willingness to ask direct questions.

The bigredbox Take

Good suppliers reduce risk. Poorly governed supplier access concentrates it.

The answer is not to bring every service in-house or distrust every provider. It is to treat third-party access with the same care as internal administrator access. Know who can reach your systems, limit what they can do, monitor important activity and plan for the day when the connection is unavailable or compromised.

If your business depends on an IT supplier but cannot explain its access, controls and recovery arrangements, that is the place to start.
