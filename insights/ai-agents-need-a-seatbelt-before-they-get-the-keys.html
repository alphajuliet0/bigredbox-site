AI Agents Need a Seatbelt Before They Get the Keys

1 June 2026

AI agents are moving from interesting demos into everyday business tools. They can summarise inboxes, draft replies, search files, update records, raise tickets and connect one system to another. That is useful. It is also a security boundary that many businesses have not defined yet.

In May 2026, the UK National Cyber Security Centre and international partners published guidance on the careful adoption of agentic AI services. The message for SMEs is not to avoid AI. The message is to stop treating an AI agent like a clever chatbot once it can take actions, access business data or use connected tools.

A chatbot answers a question. An agent may do something on your behalf. That difference matters.

Why This Matters to UK SMEs

Most smaller businesses are adopting AI in a practical way. Staff are using Microsoft Copilot, ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, CRM assistants, finance automation and support tools because they save time. That is normal business change, not a fringe experiment.

The risk appears when those tools are given broad access without the same controls you would apply to a person, supplier or administrator. If an AI agent can read customer files, send emails, change records, access finance data or interact with third-party systems, it needs governance.

The government’s latest Cyber Security Breaches Survey says 43% of UK businesses identified a cyber breach or attack in the previous 12 months. SMEs are already dealing with phishing, impersonation, weak access control and supplier exposure. Agentic AI can make those existing risks faster and harder to spot if it is introduced without guardrails.

The Real Risk Is Authority, Not Intelligence

The security question is not whether the AI is clever. The question is what it is allowed to do.

An agent with access to email can leak sensitive conversations. An agent connected to a CRM can expose client records. An agent with finance permissions can assist fraud if a malicious instruction is smuggled into a document, web page or email. An agent that uses long-lived credentials can become another route into the business if those credentials are stolen.

This is why the NCSC guidance stresses low-risk use cases, least privilege, secure defaults and human accountability. Those are not enterprise-only ideas. They are exactly the controls SMEs need because smaller teams often rely on fewer people, fewer checks and outsourced IT.

Before You Turn on an AI Agent

  • Start with a low-risk task. Use agents first for internal drafting, summarising or triage. Avoid giving them authority over payments, client communications or live business records until controls are proven.
  • List every connected system. Know which mailboxes, folders, calendars, CRM records, finance tools and ticketing systems the agent can access.
  • Apply least privilege. Give the agent only the access needed for the task. Do not reuse an owner, director or administrator account.
  • Use separate accounts and temporary credentials. An AI agent should have its own identity where possible, with access that can be revoked quickly.
  • Keep a human approval step. Require a person to approve external emails, customer updates, payment changes, data exports and deletions.
  • Log what the agent does. Keep records of prompts, data accessed, actions taken and outputs produced. If something goes wrong, you need evidence.
  • Plan the off switch. Know how to suspend the agent, revoke tokens, remove integrations and roll back changes.

Prompt Injection Is a Business Risk

Prompt injection sounds technical, but the business version is simple. The agent reads something untrusted, such as a web page, document or email, and treats hidden or malicious instructions as if they were legitimate. That can lead it to ignore rules, reveal data or take the wrong action.

For SMEs, this matters because agents are often asked to process exactly those untrusted inputs. Sales enquiries, supplier documents, CVs, invoices, customer emails and web research can all contain instructions the agent should not obey.

A sensible rule is this: never let an agent both read untrusted content and take high-impact action without human review.

Questions Directors Should Ask

  • Which AI tools are already being used by staff?
  • Which of those tools can access business data?
  • Can any AI tool send messages, change records or trigger workflows?
  • Who approved that access?
  • Can we see logs of what happened?
  • How quickly can we revoke access if there is a problem?
  • Have suppliers told us if they use AI agents on our data?

The bigredbox View

Agentic AI will be useful for SMEs, but only if it is introduced like a business system rather than a toy. The correct starting point is not a ban. It is controlled adoption.

Use AI where it removes admin burden, improves speed or helps staff make better decisions. Keep humans accountable for decisions that affect clients, money, security or legal obligations. Treat every AI integration as a new identity, a new supplier and a new access path into the business.

If your business cannot explain what an agent can access, what it can do and how to shut it down, it is not ready for that agent to hold the keys.
