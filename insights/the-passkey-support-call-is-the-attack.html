The Passkey Support Call Is the Attack

14 September 2026

A call from the IT helpdesk says your passkey or single sign-on settings must be updated. A text arrives with a link. The page looks like Microsoft, and the request feels routine.

That routine request may be the attack.

Microsoft Security Research is tracking active cloud intrusions that use passkey, multi-factor authentication and single sign-on updates as a pretext. Attackers contact staff through personal phones, SMS and sometimes trusted internal accounts. The objective is not to break Passkeys. It is to persuade the user to authorise an attacker-controlled session.

For UK SMEs, the lesson is important. Strong authentication remains one of the best protections available, but the process around enrolment, reset and support can still be manipulated.

What the Attack Looks Like

The attacker researches the organisation and the people working there. They may know the employee's name, role, manager and technology provider. They then impersonate IT support and create urgency around a passkey, MFA or SSO update.

The employee is sent to a convincing sign-in page or asked to enter a code on a genuine Microsoft page. In an adversary-in-the-middle attack, the criminal captures credentials and session tokens. In a device-code attack, the victim unknowingly approves access for the attacker's application.

The user may complete MFA correctly. That does not mean the session is safe. Authentication proves that the user approved something. It does not prove they understood who initiated it.

The First Compromise Is Only the Start

Microsoft observed attackers adding new authentication methods, accessing identity portals and using Microsoft Graph to search SharePoint, OneDrive and email. Some activity was automated, allowing criminals to collect business information at scale.

This creates several risks for an SME:

  • Customer, staff and commercial documents can be copied from cloud storage.
  • Email can be searched for invoices, contracts and useful business context.
  • A new MFA method can give the attacker a route back into the account.
  • Compromised accounts can be used to target colleagues, customers and suppliers.
  • Stolen information can support extortion, fraud or a wider ransomware attack.

The initial phone call may leave little technical evidence, especially if the link was opened on a personal mobile device. The employee's report can be the earliest and most useful signal.

Passkeys Are Still the Right Direction

This campaign does not mean Passkeys are broken. Microsoft continues to recommend phishing-resistant MFA such as FIDO2 Passkeys and Windows Hello for Business.

The weakness is social and procedural. Attackers are borrowing the language of stronger security to make their request sound legitimate. A business should continue deploying Passkeys, while making the enrolment and recovery process harder to impersonate.

Create One Trusted Support Route

Staff need to know how genuine authentication changes will be communicated. Use a known channel, such as the company helpdesk portal or an established internal number. Do not ask people to trust an unexpected call, text message or link.

Make the rule easy to remember: IT support will never call unexpectedly and ask you to follow a passkey, MFA or SSO link. If a change is required, the employee should end the contact and verify it through the published support route.

Suppliers must follow the same rule. An outsourced IT provider should not rely on caller confidence or personal information to prove identity.

Protect Authentication Registration

Adding a new passkey, authenticator app or phone number is a high-risk event. It should receive stronger controls than an ordinary sign-in.

Where the Microsoft licensing and environment allow it, require a managed device, a trusted location or a fresh phishing-resistant sign-in before security information can be registered. Alert administrators when authentication methods are added or changed, especially for directors, finance staff and privileged users.

Review existing methods regularly. Remove old phones, unused Passkeys and recovery details that no longer belong to the user.

Limit Device-Code Authentication

Device-code flows have legitimate uses, but many organisations do not need them for most users. Attackers exploit the flow because the victim enters a code on a real Microsoft page and can believe the process is safe.

Ask your IT provider whether device-code and authentication-transfer flows are allowed. Block them unless there is a documented business need. Where an exception is required, restrict it to the users, devices and applications that genuinely need it.

Make Reporting Immediate and Blame-Free

Staff should report unexpected authentication calls, messages and prompts immediately, even if they did not click. If they did follow the process, they should not waste time hiding the mistake.

The response team may need to revoke sessions and refresh tokens, reset credentials, remove attacker-added authentication methods and check mailbox rules. It should also review unusual sign-ins and high-volume access to SharePoint, OneDrive and email.

A fast report can limit the amount of information collected. A delayed report gives the attacker time to establish persistence and learn how the business operates.

Questions to Ask Your IT Provider

  • How will staff recognise a genuine passkey or MFA enrolment request?
  • Can authentication methods be added from unmanaged devices?
  • Are administrators alerted when a new method is registered?
  • Are device-code flows blocked unless explicitly required?
  • Can we identify and revoke every active session for a compromised user?
  • Do we log Microsoft Graph, SharePoint, OneDrive and mailbox activity?
  • What is the verified route for reporting a suspicious support call?
  • How quickly can you investigate an unexpected MFA change?

The bigredbox Take

Passkeys make authentication stronger. Attackers know that, so they are turning the upgrade process into a believable story.

The answer is not to slow down passkey adoption. It is to secure the human and technical processes around it. Give staff one trusted support route, protect authentication registration, restrict unnecessary device-code flows and treat every new MFA method as a security event.

If someone calls unexpectedly and asks you to fix your authentication, stop. Verify the person before you verify the device.
