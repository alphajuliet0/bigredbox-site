AI Will Make Cyber Attacks Faster, Not Magic

22 June 2026

Five Eyes cyber agencies are warning that advanced AI could change the speed, scale and complexity of cyber attacks within months. That sounds dramatic, but the practical message for UK SMEs is more grounded: the same weaknesses will still be exploited, but attackers may find them faster, write better lures and move through decisions more quickly.

The risk is not that every small business will be targeted by a super-intelligent hacking system tomorrow. The risk is that ordinary weaknesses become easier to discover and abuse at volume.

Weak passwords, exposed remote access, delayed patching, poor backups and unclear incident roles already cause serious damage. AI does not need to invent a new type of attack if it can help criminals scale the old ones.

What Has Changed?

Attackers have always looked for speed and repeatability. AI helps with both. It can support reconnaissance, summarise stolen data, generate convincing phishing emails, adapt messages for different sectors and help less skilled criminals follow attack playbooks.

That does not make criminals unstoppable. It does mean defenders need less theatre and more discipline. The businesses that recover best are not usually the ones with the most complex tools. They are the ones that know what they own, control access tightly, patch quickly, keep tested backups and know who makes decisions when something breaks.

Why This Matters for SMEs

Large organisations may have security teams, monitoring platforms and formal crisis structures. Many SMEs do not. That creates a timing problem. If an attack develops faster, the business cannot afford to spend the first day working out who owns Microsoft 365, where the backups are, who can speak to the insurer or whether the IT supplier has admin access.

AI changes the tempo. Your response process needs to be clear before you need it.

The Four Areas to Fix First

1. Identity

Most business compromise still begins with access. Email, cloud storage, finance systems and remote support accounts are high-value targets. Multi-factor authentication should be enforced wherever possible, but not all MFA is equal. Push fatigue, shared accounts and weak recovery processes can still be abused.

Start with administrator accounts, finance users, directors and anyone who can change email rules, payments, backups or security settings. Remove old accounts and avoid shared credentials. If Passkeys are available for critical services, use them.

2. Patching

AI-assisted discovery makes known vulnerabilities more dangerous because attackers can search and prioritise exposed systems more quickly. You do not need a perfect patching programme to reduce risk, but you do need a visible one.

Keep a simple list of internet-facing services, firewalls, VPNs, remote access tools, websites, plug-ins and cloud platforms. Decide who watches vendor alerts, who applies updates and how quickly critical fixes must be applied.

3. Backups

Backups are not a storage task. They are a recovery capability. A backup that has never been restored is an assumption, not a control.

Check that important data is backed up, separated from everyday administrator access and tested regularly. Make sure someone knows how to restore key systems if the normal laptop, server or cloud account is unavailable.

4. Decision-Making

During an incident, delay is expensive. SMEs should have a one-page decision plan that names the person who can authorise containment, contact the IT provider, speak to the insurer, notify customers if required and decide whether systems should be taken offline.

This does not need to be complicated. It needs to be available when email and chat are not.

What to Ask Your IT Provider This Week

  • Which accounts have administrator access to our systems?
  • Where is MFA enforced, and where is it still missing?
  • Which systems are exposed to the internet?
  • How quickly are critical patches applied?
  • When did we last test a restore from backup?
  • What logs would we use to investigate suspicious access?
  • Who do we call first if email or cloud services are compromised?

If the answers are vague, that is not a reason to panic. It is a reason to document the position and close the most important gaps.

Do Not Buy Before You Can Operate

AI security products will be marketed aggressively because the threat is real and the language is persuasive. Some tools will be useful. Some will add noise. Buying more technology before fixing basic governance can make the environment harder to understand.

Before adding another tool, make sure your existing controls are working. Can you see sign-ins? Can you disable a compromised account quickly? Can you restore data? Can you prove which devices are managed? Can your supplier explain their access?

If not, start there.

The bigredbox Take

AI is going to make cyber attacks faster. It will not make the basics irrelevant.

For SMEs, the priority is not to predict every future AI capability. The priority is to remove easy access, reduce exposed systems, test recovery and make incident decisions faster. That is what turns a cyber scare into a manageable business problem.

The businesses that prepare now will not avoid every incident. They will lose less time when one arrives.
