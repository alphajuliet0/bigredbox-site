The Invoice Looks Right. The Approval Process Is Wrong

21 September 2026

An email appears to come from the managing director. It includes a professional invoice, a convincing forwarded conversation and a request for payment. The amount is plausible. The supplier looks familiar. The instruction feels urgent but not unusual.

The problem is that none of it is real.

Microsoft Security Research recently detected a campaign that sent more than one million financial fraud emails. Attackers impersonated senior executives, created lookalike supplier domains and used fabricated invoices and email threads to persuade finance teams to make payments. Microsoft also found indicators consistent with generative AI being used to help build and personalise the campaign templates.

For UK SMEs, the useful lesson is not that every polished invoice is suspicious. It is that visual quality, familiar names and professional language are no longer reliable evidence of authenticity. Payment security has to depend on a process that remains safe when the email itself looks perfect.

What Made the Campaign Convincing

The attackers did not rely on one obvious trick. They combined several believable elements into a single story:

  • An executive name appeared in the sender details and signature.
  • A recognised supplier was impersonated through a lookalike domain.
  • The invoice included realistic dates, line items, payment details and company information.
  • A fabricated forwarded conversation appeared to show prior approval.
  • The recipient was encouraged to act without copying other people into the conversation.

Each detail reduced the chance that the recipient would pause. Together, they created the appearance of an established decision rather than a new request requiring verification.

AI Improves Presentation, Not the Criminal's Authority

Generative AI can help criminals produce cleaner layouts, more natural language and tailored messages at scale. It can also reduce the spelling and grammar mistakes that staff have traditionally been told to look for.

That does not make the fraud technically sophisticated. The criminal still needs a person inside the business to accept the story and release the money. The best defence is therefore not a better spelling test. It is a payment process that does not treat email as sufficient authority.

Separate the Request from the Approval

No payment, bank-detail change or urgent transfer should be approved solely within the email thread that requested it. Verification must move to a separate, trusted route.

If a director appears to request a payment, contact them through a known phone number, approved messaging channel or face-to-face conversation. Do not use the telephone number in the email, invoice or signature. If supplier bank details change, call a known contact using information already held in your finance system.

The aim is simple: the attacker should not be able to control both the request and the verification.

Use Two-Person Approval for Meaningful Payments

A second approver creates time and independence. Set sensible thresholds based on the size of the business, but include exceptions for any new supplier, changed bank account, unusual payment route or request that bypasses normal timing.

The second check must be more than another click in the same compromised mailbox. The approver should see the supplier record, purchase order or contract, evidence of independent verification and the destination bank details.

Make Supplier Changes a Security Event

Changes to bank details, contact information or payment instructions should be logged, verified and reviewed. Staff should know that a genuine supplier will accept a short delay while the business validates a change.

Use a standard callback procedure. Record who was contacted, which known number was used, who approved the change and when it took effect. Where possible, configure the banking platform to alert more than one person when a new beneficiary is created.

Improve Email Controls, but Do Not Depend on Them

SPF, DKIM and DMARC help reduce direct domain spoofing. Anti-phishing controls can identify display-name impersonation, suspicious reply-to addresses, recently registered domains and known malicious infrastructure.

These controls matter, but attackers can use legitimate third-party email services and newly registered lookalike domains. Finance procedures must still work when a message passes technical filtering.

Give Finance Teams Permission to Slow Down

Urgency and authority are central to invoice fraud. Staff need explicit permission to pause a payment, challenge a senior person and verify an instruction without being criticised for causing delay.

Use short exercises based on realistic invoices rather than generic phishing examples. Include directors and senior managers. If leaders ignore the process when they are busy, attackers will use that exception as part of the story.

Questions to Ask This Week

  • Can one person create and approve a new beneficiary?
  • How are supplier bank-detail changes independently verified?
  • What payment value always requires a second approver?
  • Would finance staff challenge an urgent request from a director?
  • Are lookalike domains and display-name impersonation monitored?
  • Are SPF, DKIM and DMARC correctly configured for every company domain?
  • Can the bank delay or recall a suspicious payment?
  • Who must be contacted immediately if fraud is suspected?

The bigredbox Take

AI can make a fraudulent request look more professional, but it cannot make it authorised.

Do not design payment security around spotting bad grammar or unusual formatting. Build it around independent verification, two-person approval and controlled supplier changes. A convincing email should be able to start a check, never complete one.

If the request controls the route used to verify it, the verification is not independent.
