Your Router Is Now Part of the Attack Surface

18 May 2026

Most SME cyber plans focus on laptops, email and cloud apps. That is sensible, but it can miss the one device that everything depends on: the internet router.

On 7 April 2026, the UK National Cyber Security Centre published an advisory on APT28 exploiting vulnerable routers to change DNS settings and redirect traffic through attacker controlled infrastructure. In plain English, attackers can interfere with where staff devices are sent online, then use that position to steal passwords, access tokens and other login material.

This is not only an enterprise problem. The warning specifically covers small office and home office routers. That matters for UK SMEs because many businesses still rely on ageing broadband routers, unmanaged firewalls, home working kit and supplier installed devices that nobody has reviewed for years.

Why This Matters to UK SMEs

A compromised router does not need to break into every laptop. It sits in the path between your people and the services they use every day. Email, Microsoft 365, accounting platforms, CRM systems, payment portals and supplier logins all depend on trusted network routing.

DNS hijacking is especially dangerous because it can look like a normal login journey. A member of staff opens a browser, visits a familiar service and may only see a certificate warning, a strange redirect or a login prompt that feels slightly off. If credentials or session tokens are captured, multi-factor authentication can still reduce the blast radius, but it is not a reason to ignore the router.

The commercial risk is straightforward: account takeover, invoice fraud, stolen mailboxes, data exposure and business interruption. For a smaller business, one compromised admin account can be enough to stop work, damage client trust or trigger an expensive incident response.

The Routers to Worry About First

Start with the devices that are internet facing, forgotten or outside normal IT management. That usually includes broadband routers, Wi-Fi access points, small firewalls, VPN appliances, home office routers used by senior staff, warehouse or branch office equipment and anything installed by a telecoms provider or legacy IT supplier.

Old does not automatically mean compromised, but unsupported equipment is a business risk. If a device no longer receives security updates, it should not be protecting the front door of the company.

What to Check This Week

  • Find every edge device. Build a simple list of routers, firewalls, VPN appliances and wireless controllers. Include the location, owner, model, support status and who has admin access.
  • Change default or shared admin passwords. Use unique credentials stored in a password manager. Remove old supplier accounts that are no longer needed.
  • Disable remote management from the internet. Admin interfaces should not be publicly reachable unless there is a very specific, controlled reason.
  • Check DNS settings. Confirm the router is using expected DNS resolvers. Unexpected IP addresses, especially ones nobody can explain, should be investigated.
  • Update firmware. Apply vendor updates promptly. If updates are no longer available, plan replacement rather than accepting permanent exposure.
  • Protect cloud accounts with MFA or Passkeys. Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, finance systems, remote access and admin portals should all have strong multi-factor authentication.
  • Log enough to investigate. Keep router, firewall and DNS logs where possible. If something goes wrong, evidence matters.

Questions for Your IT Provider

If your IT is outsourced, this is a good moment to ask direct questions. Which routers and firewalls do we own? Are any out of support? Are management interfaces exposed to the internet? When were passwords last rotated? Who monitors DNS changes? What would you check if we suspected traffic was being redirected?

These are not trick questions. They are basic governance questions. A good provider should be able to answer them clearly and show evidence.

The bigredbox View

For many SMEs, router security is treated as a setup task rather than an ongoing control. That needs to change. The router, firewall and DNS layer are part of the business attack surface, just like email and endpoints.

The practical answer is not panic. It is ownership. Know what equipment you have, keep it supported, remove unsafe access, monitor changes and make sure critical accounts are protected if something at the network edge is abused.

Cyber Essentials remains a sensible baseline because it forces the right conversations around firewalls, secure configuration, software updates, user access and malware protection. For businesses that handle sensitive client data or depend heavily on cloud services, those basics should be treated as board level controls, not technical housekeeping.
