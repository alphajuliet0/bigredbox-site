The Computers You Forgot Were Computers

31 August 2026

Your CCTV recorder is a computer. So is the door-entry controller, the heating system, the warehouse scanner, the production-line controller and the box that lets an engineer provide remote support.

They may not look like laptops, but they run software, use accounts and connect to networks. If they are exposed to the internet, use default passwords or rely on obsolete equipment, they can also be attacked.

The NCSC issued a warning on 27 August after seeing increased targeting of operational technology across several sectors, including in the UK. Some activity caused limited real-world disruption. The message for smaller organisations is clear: do not assume that physical systems are private simply because nobody intended to make them public.

What Operational Technology Means for an SME

Operational technology, usually shortened to OT, controls or monitors physical processes. In a factory it may run machinery. In an office, shop, school, warehouse or care setting it can include access control, CCTV, alarms, heating, ventilation, refrigeration, lighting, lifts, energy monitoring and building-management systems.

Many of these systems were installed by facilities contractors rather than the IT team. They may have been configured once, connected for remote maintenance and then left alone for years. That separation creates a dangerous gap: IT assumes the installer manages the equipment, while the installer assumes the customer manages the network.

An attacker does not care which department owns the system. They care whether they can reach it.

How Exposure Happens

The NCSC warns that unintended internet exposure can arise through misconfiguration, legacy connections and unmanaged assets. In practice, that can mean a router port opened for an engineer, an old remote-access appliance, a web interface protected only by a default password or a controller connected directly to the public internet.

Remote access is often added for a sensible business reason. It saves call-out time and lets a supplier diagnose faults quickly. The risk appears when the connection is not recorded, restricted, monitored or removed when the contract changes.

Older operational equipment can make the problem harder. It may run for far longer than ordinary office technology, use insecure protocols or no longer receive security updates. Replacing it may require downtime, engineering work and capital spending, so weaknesses remain in place.

Why This Is a Business Risk

A compromise does not need to steal customer records to be serious. It could unlock doors, disable cameras, interrupt production, alter environmental controls or force a business to stop operations while it investigates.

Operational systems also create a route into other parts of the organisation when they share a network with staff devices and servers. A weakly protected controller can become an initial foothold, while a compromised business laptop may provide a route in the other direction.

The commercial consequences are familiar: downtime, missed orders, safety concerns, emergency engineering costs, reputational damage and difficult conversations with customers or insurers.

What to Do Now

1. Identify Every Connected Physical System

Walk the premises with whoever manages IT, facilities and maintenance. List systems that control, monitor or record physical activity. Include the device, its purpose, location, network connection, supplier, support status and named business owner.

Ask suppliers directly whether their equipment can be reached remotely, how that access works and who holds the credentials. Do not accept vague assurances that a system is secure because it is specialist.

2. Verify What Is Exposed

Do not rely on assumptions or an old network diagram. Ask your IT provider to confirm which services and management interfaces are visible from the internet. Check firewalls, routers, remote-access tools and any connections created for third-party support.

UK organisations can also register domains and public IP addresses with the NCSC's free Early Warning service. It provides notifications about potential compromises, vulnerabilities and open ports identified through its information feeds.

3. Remove Direct Internet Access

Operational devices should not be directly accessible from the public internet. Where remote support is genuinely required, route it through a controlled access point with strong authentication, logging and time-limited access.

Change default credentials, remove shared administrator passwords and enable multi-factor authentication wherever the system supports it. If a supplier says these controls are impossible, record the limitation and agree a compensating control rather than ignoring it.

4. Separate Operational and Office Networks

CCTV, building controls and machinery should not sit on the same unrestricted network as laptops, guest Wi-Fi and ordinary business systems. Segmentation limits the routes available to an attacker and reduces the chance that one compromised device affects everything else.

Only permit the communications each system actually needs. Ask for unexpected connections and configuration changes to be logged and reviewed.

5. Retire Unsupported Equipment

Record the make, model, software version and support end date for critical devices. Apply vendor security updates promptly and plan replacement before boundary equipment, controllers and remote-access appliances reach end of life.

If immediate replacement is not practical, reduce exposure, isolate the system and monitor it more closely. A known legacy risk needs an owner, a deadline and a funded plan.

6. Prepare for Safe Manual Operation

Back up configurations, controller logic and engineering information, then test whether they can be restored. Document how to isolate affected systems and how the business would operate safely if automation, access control or monitoring became unavailable.

This is not only an IT recovery exercise. Facilities, operations, suppliers and senior management should understand their roles before an incident.

Questions to Ask Your Suppliers

  • Is this equipment accessible from outside our premises?
  • Which accounts exist, and are any passwords shared or still set to defaults?
  • Does remote support require our approval each time?
  • Are connections logged, and who reviews those logs?
  • When does the equipment stop receiving security updates?
  • Can the system be isolated without creating a safety problem?
  • How quickly could you restore its configuration after an incident?

The bigredbox Take

The difficult part of OT security is often not advanced hacking. It is discovering a connection that nobody remembered approving and finding equipment that sits between IT, facilities and an external supplier.

Start with visibility. Find the systems that affect the physical operation of your business, confirm who can reach them and remove unnecessary internet access. Treat every connected controller as a computer, because attackers already do.
