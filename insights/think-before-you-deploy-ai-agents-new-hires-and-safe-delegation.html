Think Before You Deploy: AI Agents, New Hires and Safe Delegation

28 May 2026

The NCSC’s latest warning on agentic AI is aimed at organisations thinking about giving AI systems more autonomy. The advice is simple: start small, limit access, monitor behaviour and keep humans accountable.

That is good cyber advice, but it is also good management advice. In the current UK business climate, the same thinking applies when you hire real people. Whether you are deploying an AI agent or bringing in a new member of staff, you are giving someone, or something, the ability to act on behalf of your business.

Why This Matters to UK SMEs

SMEs are under pressure to do more with less. Hiring is cautious, costs are high, and employment law changes mean businesses need stronger early-stage management. ACAS says unfair dismissal protection will become available after six months in a job from 1 January 2027, rather than after two years. That makes probation, documentation and performance management more important, not less.

At the same time, AI tools are being sold as a shortcut to productivity. Agentic AI goes further than ordinary chatbots. The NCSC explains that these systems can access data, remember context, use tools, make decisions and take actions towards a goal. That can be useful, but it can also make behaviour harder to predict, test and govern.

The common theme is delegation. Delegation creates value only when the task, authority, safeguards and review process are clear.

Do Not Confuse Capability with Readiness

A capable person can still be the wrong hire. A capable AI system can still be the wrong deployment. The question is not just “can it do the job?” The better question is “can we safely let it do this job, in this business, with this level of access?”

Before hiring or deploying, define the role properly. What outcome do you need? What decisions are included? What decisions are excluded? What data, systems, customers or suppliers will be touched? What could go wrong if the person or agent misunderstands the instruction, takes a shortcut, or acts outside the intended scope?

This is especially important where the role involves money, customer data, staff records, contracts, suppliers, payroll, security settings or public communications. Those are not good places for vague job descriptions, vague prompts or unchecked autonomy.

Start with Bounded Pilots and Probation

The NCSC recommends starting agentic AI in low-risk, tightly bounded pilots before expanding scope. SMEs should take the same approach with hiring.

A probation period should not be a calendar event where everyone waits five months and then has an awkward meeting. It should be an active risk-control process. Set 30, 60 and 90-day expectations. Give clear examples of acceptable work. Check whether the person is following process, handling data correctly, escalating concerns and communicating honestly.

For AI agents, run the same pattern. Start with read-only access where possible. Let the tool recommend actions before it is allowed to execute them. Test it with real-world edge cases. Keep its first tasks repetitive, low-risk and easy to reverse. Do not connect it to sensitive systems until you know how it behaves.

Apply Least Privilege to People Too

Cyber security teams talk about “least privilege”, which means giving the minimum access needed for the shortest necessary time. It applies just as well to people.

New starters should not automatically inherit broad access because “that is what the last person had”. AI agents should not get administrator rights because it makes integration easier. Temporary staff, contractors, managers and finance users all need access that matches their actual responsibilities.

Practical safeguards include named user accounts, multi-factor authentication, role-based access, approval limits, segregation of duties, audit logging and prompt removal of access when someone changes role or leaves. For AI systems, add clear limits on which tools they can use, what actions need human approval and when credentials expire.

Review Performance and Safety Regularly

Performance reviews should not just ask “is output increasing?” They should ask “is the work being done safely?”

For employees, review judgement, data handling, customer impact, policy compliance and escalation habits. For AI agents, review logs, exceptions, failed actions, unusual activity, user complaints and any outputs that required correction. In both cases, look for drift. A person may gradually work around a process. An AI workflow may expand beyond its original purpose. A supplier integration may change. A once-safe setup can become unsafe over time.

Regular reviews also protect the business commercially. They create evidence, improve training, show whether safeguards are working and help managers intervene before a small issue becomes a serious incident.

Keep Accountability Human

The NCSC is clear that humans remain accountable for the decision to deploy an agent, the access it is given, the safeguards around it and the consequences of its operation. The same is true of recruitment and management.

“The employee did it” is rarely a complete answer if the business gave unclear instructions, poor training, excessive access or no supervision. “The AI did it” is even weaker. Somebody must own the decision, approve the access, monitor the activity and have the authority to stop the work.

The bigredbox Take

AI agents and employees are not the same, but the management lesson is similar. Do not delegate blindly. Define the job, limit the authority, monitor the behaviour, review the risk and keep a named person accountable.

For UK SMEs, this is not about slowing progress. It is about making change safer. A good hire can transform a business. A well-controlled AI agent can remove repetitive work and improve service. Both need structure before trust.
