If the Cloud Stops, Does Your Business Stop Too?

13 July 2026

UK regulators are increasing scrutiny of the major cloud providers that support critical financial services. That may sound like a banking-sector story, but the lesson applies directly to SMEs: cloud services are now part of the operating fabric of almost every business.

Email, files, accounting, CRM, telephony, websites, backups, payment tools and AI services often sit with a small number of external providers. That creates efficiency, but it also creates concentration. If one service fails, changes terms, locks an account, suffers an incident or becomes unavailable, the business can stop moving very quickly.

The point is not to avoid cloud services. The point is to stop treating cloud availability as someone else's problem.

Cloud Is Not a Resilience Plan

Many businesses assume that because a service is cloud-based, it is automatically backed up, recoverable and always available. That assumption is risky. A provider may run excellent infrastructure while the customer still has weak account security, poor data exports, no tested recovery process and no clear outage decision plan.

Cloud providers are responsible for their platforms. You remain responsible for how your business uses them, who can access them, what data is stored there and how work continues when they are unavailable.

Where SMEs Are Most Exposed

The highest-risk cloud dependencies are usually the ones that quietly became business-critical:

  • Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace for email, documents and identity;
  • Finance and payroll platforms;
  • CRM and ticketing systems;
  • Website hosting, DNS and domain management;
  • Cloud backup and file sharing;
  • Remote monitoring or IT support tools;
  • AI tools connected to business data;
  • Single sign-on systems used to access several other services.

If one of these fails, the impact is not only technical. It can stop invoicing, delay payroll, block customer communication, interrupt support and prevent directors from making informed decisions.

Five Questions to Ask This Week

1. What Services Would Stop Us Trading?

Make a short list of the cloud services that the business genuinely depends on. Do not make this an IT asset exercise. Ask the operational question: if this service was unavailable for one working day, what would stop?

2. Who Owns Each Service?

Every critical cloud service needs a named business owner and a technical owner. The business owner understands impact and priority. The technical owner understands accounts, configuration, support routes and recovery options.

3. Can We Export Our Data?

Knowing that data is stored in a cloud platform is not enough. Check whether you can export it in a usable format, how long export takes and who has permission to do it. If the only person who knows is an external supplier, document the process.

4. What Happens If Login Fails?

Cloud outages are not the only problem. Account lockouts, conditional access mistakes, lost MFA devices and compromised administrators can have the same effect. Confirm who can recover access and how emergency access is protected.

5. Have We Tested Recovery?

A backup that has never been restored is only a theory. Test at least one practical recovery scenario: restoring a deleted mailbox, exporting customer data, recovering a finance report or accessing key files without the normal user account.

Do Not Confuse Backup with Continuity

Backups protect data. Continuity keeps the business working. You need both.

For example, if your email platform is down, a mailbox backup may not help staff communicate with customers that morning. You may need an alternative contact process, a status page, a phone script, access to key customer records and authority to make decisions without waiting for email to return.

For SMEs, continuity can be simple. A one-page plan is better than a large document nobody reads. It should say:

  • Which services are critical;
  • Who makes decisions during an outage;
  • How staff will be contacted if email or chat is unavailable;
  • How customers will be updated;
  • Where offline copies of essential contacts and procedures are kept;
  • Which supplier support routes should be used;
  • When to trigger cyber insurance or incident support.

Security Still Matters

Cloud resilience is not only about outages. Many cloud disruptions begin with account compromise, poor permissions or supplier access. Strong identity controls reduce the chance that a criminal, disgruntled insider or accidental misconfiguration turns a cloud platform into a single point of failure.

Prioritise administrator accounts, finance users and anyone who can change domains, email rules, backup settings or security policies. Enforce MFA, remove old users, review third-party app access and separate privileged accounts from everyday email use.

Ask Better Supplier Questions

If an IT provider manages your cloud estate, ask for plain answers:

  • Which cloud services are critical to our business?
  • Where are the admin accounts, and who controls them?
  • Can we export our data without your help?
  • What backup and restore tests have been completed?
  • What happens if your team is unavailable during an outage?
  • How quickly would you tell us about a provider incident affecting us?
  • What access do your own suppliers have to our environment?

A good provider should welcome these questions. They are not accusations. They are resilience basics.

The bigredbox Take

The cloud can make a business stronger, but only if reliance is understood and managed. Convenience is not the same as resilience.

UK regulators are looking closely at major cloud providers because the concentration risk is real. SMEs do not need a regulatory framework to act on the same lesson. Know what you depend on, protect access, test recovery and write down how the business continues when a critical service fails.

If one cloud login, one supplier or one platform can stop the business, that is not just an IT issue. It is a board-level risk.
